**Description**

```
Paris, 1870. Camille Jordan publishes a 667-page treatise on substitutions, and in it a simplest form for every linear map. In 1874 Leopold Kronecker attacks it in public: Weierstrass had the same classification in 1868, he says, and did it properly. The quarrel is over what's left when a map doesn't have enough eigenvectors.

Season 2, episode 13 of Whyme Labs' math series. It fails on a deck of cards. A shear keeps horizontal arrows put, eigenvalue 1, but there's no second eigen-direction: the eigenvalue counts twice and owns one line. Look at S − I instead: it sends the vertical arrow to the horizontal one and the horizontal one to zero, so the vertical arrow is a generalized eigenvector, an eigenvector after two steps. Jordan's theorem: on a complex space every map splits into blocks, each with one eigenvalue down the diagonal and ones just above it, the nudge that pushes each rung of a ladder down until it falls to zero. The payoff: a damped spring. Too little damping and it swings, too much and it creeps; at the exact balance the two eigenvalues merge, the matrix becomes a Jordan block, and a new term appears, t·e⁻ᵗ: critical damping.
```

```
Some linear maps, like a simple shear, don't have enough eigenvectors to diagonalize. Jordan's 1870 treatise (and Weierstrass's 1868 elementary divisors, the subject of a famous priority quarrel) shows what every map looks like instead: blocks with one eigenvalue on the diagonal and ones just above it.
```
