**Description**

```
The polynomials 1, 1 + x, 1 + x + x²/2, … crowd closer and closer together. They're heading somewhere, and that somewhere is eˣ, which isn't a polynomial at all. Inside the polynomials, the sequence has nowhere to land. The same thing happens to 1, 3/2, 7/5, … in the fractions: they close in on √2, a hole in ℚ. A space with no holes is called complete.

Season 2, episode 23 of Whyme Labs' math series. A Cauchy sequence is one whose terms end up as close together as you like. A space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges, and a complete normed space is a Banach space. ℝⁿ, ℓᵖ, C[0, 1] with the peak norm and Lᵖ are complete; the polynomials, the finite sequences and C[0, 1] with the area norm are not. We prove ℓ² is complete coordinate by coordinate, see that every space has a completion (ℚ becomes ℝ), and show why absolutely convergent series need completeness. The payoff is Banach's contraction mapping theorem: a map that shrinks distances by a factor q < 1 has exactly one fixed point, found by iterating. It solves cos x = x, proves Picard's existence theorem for y′ = y, and runs PageRank.
```
