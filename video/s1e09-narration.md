## Cold open

1. 0.999 repeating equals one. It may be the most argued-about equation on the internet.
2. One side says it gets closer and closer but never arrives. The other side has tricks: three thirds, or ten x minus x.
3. The skeptics are asking the right question. Nobody can write infinitely many nines, so what does 0.999 repeating even name?

## The staircase of truncations

1. Start with what we can write: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999. Each one is short of one by exactly one unit in its last place.
2. Zoom in ten times and the picture looks the same. Every one of these truncations is below one.
3. But 0.999 repeating is none of them. It's supposed to be a single number, not a process.

## A decimal names a least upper bound

1. Here's the definition. A decimal names the least upper bound of its truncations, which exists because the reals are complete.
2. So lower a ceiling onto the staircase and stop at the lowest spot that's still above every step. It stops at one.

## No room below 1

1. Could the ceiling stop a little lower, at some c below one? Then every step would have to stay under c.
2. Pick c close to one. The steps march past it. Pick c even closer, and they march past again.
3. If 0.999 repeating were less than one, the gap would be smaller than every ten to the minus n. That's an infinitesimal.

## No infinitesimals in ℝ

1. And the reals have none. If the whole numbers had a least upper bound b, some n would sit above b minus one.
2. Then n plus one is above b. So no ceiling stops the whole numbers, and no positive real is smaller than every one over n.

## Two names, one point

1. So 0.999 repeating and one are two names for the same point. They aren't the only pair: 0.2 and 0.1999 repeating are twins too.
2. Every decimal that stops has a twin ending in nines. Every other number, like one third, has exactly one name.

## What the tricks need

1. And the ten x minus x trick? It works here because 0.999 repeating is a real number to begin with.
2. Try it on one plus two plus four plus eight, and it says the sum is minus one. Those partial sums grow without bound.
3. A trick is a shortcut once you know the decimal names a real number. It can't prove that it does.
