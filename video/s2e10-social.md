**Description**

```
Zurich, 1912. Albert Einstein is stuck on gravity and turns to his old classmate Marcel Grossmann: help me, or I'll go crazy. Grossmann finds the tool in a memoir by Ricci and Levi-Civita: grids of numbers that change by strict rules when the coordinates change. Physicists still define them like this: a tensor is something that transforms like a tensor.

Season 2, episode 10 of Whyme Labs' math series. Today we define a tensor by what it does. Put a funnel ⊗ in front of a bilinear machine: every pair v, w goes in and comes out as one object, v ⊗ w, and the two-input machine becomes an ordinary linear one. With bases e₁, e₂ and f₁, f₂, f₃, the six products eᵢ ⊗ fⱼ are a basis: side by side, dimensions add (2 + 3); tensored, they multiply (2 × 3). A pure tensor is a column times a row, a grid of rank one; the identity can't be split that way, and in quantum mechanics the tensors that won't split are entangled states. The payoff: draw a tensor with vector legs and covector legs. Change the basis and each vector leg picks up P⁻¹, each covector leg P. A map has one of each, P⁻¹AP; a form has two covector legs, PᵀBP. Nobody chose these rules. The legs did.
```
