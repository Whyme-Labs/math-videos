**Description**

```
London, 1821. Charles Babbage and John Herschel are checking a book of astronomical tables, and it is full of mistakes. "I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam," Babbage says. So he designs a machine that can only add, and it works because taking differences respects adding and scaling.

Season 2, episode 3 of Whyme Labs' math series. The test for a linear map: turn a sum, or add the turned arrows, and get the same answer; same for stretching. Rotations, shadows, derivatives and shifts pass; squaring fails. That test is strong: tell a linear map where the basis arrows go and the whole grid has to follow. A noon shadow flattens space onto the floor; the vertical line it crushes to zero is the kernel, the floor it covers is the image, and 3 = 1 + 2: rank plus nullity is always the dimension you started with. Then the payoff: the differences of the squares are 1, 3, 5, 7, their differences are all 2, and the engine adds its way back up to 25, 36, 49.
```
