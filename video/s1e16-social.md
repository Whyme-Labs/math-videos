```
Close your eyes. Your friend picks up a square tile, does something to it, and puts it back exactly in its outline. Open them: nothing looks different. Did they turn it, flip it, or do nothing? There are eight moves, and you can't tell them apart. The same game works on a clock and on a Rubik's cube.

Episode 16 of "What Is a Number?". The moves in all three worlds follow the same four rules: a move followed by a move is a move, doing nothing is a move, every move can be undone, and grouping a long chain doesn't matter. That's a group. The whole numbers under adding fail the undo rule (which is why episode 6 built the integers), and subtraction fails the grouping rule. On the square, order matters: flip then turn is not turn then flip, so undoing goes in reverse, like socks and shoes. And counting itself is a group: the integers are what one undoable move builds when nothing else is assumed.
```

```
Episode 16 of our math series: groups.

It starts with a game. Close your eyes while a friend moves a square tile and puts it back in its outline. There are eight possible moves, and they're indistinguishable afterwards. The same game works on a clock and on a Rubik's cube (about 43 quintillion scrambles).

Those three worlds share four rules: moves compose, doing nothing counts, every move can be undone, and grouping doesn't matter. That's the definition of a group, one of the most useful ideas in mathematics, physics and computer science. It says nothing about what the moves are made of, only how they behave.
```
