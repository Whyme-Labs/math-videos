```
An exam hall. A student finds the antiderivative of 2x, writes x², and the teacher circles it in red: you forgot + C. Why is it there? Because x², x² + 7 and x² − π all have the same derivative. What if we declared that whole family to be one object?

Season 2, episode 5 of Whyme Labs' math series. The union of two lines isn't a space, but their sum is the whole plane. Split the plane along the x axis and one other line and every arrow breaks into two pieces, but which other line? Every choice works and none is the right one. So don't choose: glue together everything that differs by a step along the axis, and each horizontal line becomes one point. That's the quotient space, and adding its points doesn't depend on which arrows you pick. The payoff: differentiation crushes the constants, its kernel. Glue the kernel away and differentiation can be undone, and undoing it on 2x lands on the whole family x² + C. The + C was never sloppiness; it's a point of the quotient. Last, linear maps themselves form a vector space: maps from the plane into space are six dials, six dimensions.
```

**Thread** (4 posts):

1. `Why does every antiderivative need a + C? Linear algebra has a clean answer. 🧵`
2. `Differentiation sends every constant to 0. The constants are its kernel, so it can't be undone: x² and x² + 7 look identical to it.`
3. `Fix: glue together everything that differs by a constant. Each family x² + C becomes one point of a new space, the quotient. On it, differentiation is one-to-one.`
4. `So ∫ 2x dx isn't "x² plus some sloppy constant". It's a single point: the coset x² + C. <YouTube link>`
