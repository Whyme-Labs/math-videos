**Description**

```
Edo, 1683. Seki Takakazu, an accounts official for a samurai lord, writes down a rule that decides when a system of equations can be solved. Ten years later in Hanover, Leibniz finds the same rule and writes it to L'Hôpital. Neither knows about the other. Today it's the determinant: six terms for 3 × 3, twenty-four for 4 × 4, more than three million for 10 × 10.

Season 2, episode 11 of Whyme Labs' math series. Behind the pile of terms is one question: how much does a map stretch volume, and does it turn space inside out? Ask for a signed area of two arrows: stretch an arrow and the area stretches, slide one along the other and nothing changes, swap them and the sign flips, make them parallel and it's zero. Those rules fix the area. The wedge v ∧ w is an oriented patch; in n dimensions the top wedge has one direction, so a linear map can only multiply it by one number: the determinant. Push the unit cube through the map and its new volume is the determinant; turn it inside out and it goes negative; crush it flat and it's zero. Then the old miracle is obvious: scale by 2, then by 3, and you've scaled by 6, so det(AB) = det A · det B. Expand the wedge of the three columns and the six-term formula falls out, signs and all.
```
