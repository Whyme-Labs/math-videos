**Thread** (4 posts):

1. `Fractions are dense: between any two there's another, their average. So the rational number line should have no gaps. It's full of them. 🧵`
2. `Suppose a/b = √2 with b as small as possible. Then a² = 2b², so a is even. Write a = 2c, and b² = 2c², so b is even too. Now c/d is the same fraction with a smaller bottom. Contradiction: no fraction squares to 2.`
3. `Here's what the hole looks like. Take every fraction whose square is below 2. The rule q → (2q + 2)/(q + 2) turns any of them into a bigger one that's still below. Turn it on the upper bounds and they shrink forever. Nothing sits in between.`
4. `Or average x with 2/x and repeat: 1, 3/2, 17/12, 577/408. After four steps the terms agree to within a few millionths, but the limit would have to square to 2. It has nowhere to land. Episode 7: <YouTube link>`

## LinkedIn (Whyme Labs page, English)

```
Episode 7 of our math series: the holes in the rational numbers.

Fractions are dense, so between any two of them there's always another. It's natural to think that makes the number line solid. This episode shows why it doesn't: no fraction squares to 2, and the same gap appears at √3, √5 and infinitely many other points.

We look at the gap three ways. There's the classic proof by descent. There's a set of fractions with no least upper bound, where one simple rule keeps improving the guesses on both sides without ever closing the gap. And there's the Babylonian square-root sequence, which a clay tablet from around 1800–1600 BCE already gets right to five decimal places, crowding toward a limit that isn't there.

The Chinese edition adds the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, which calls such roots "开之不尽" (they never come out exactly), with Liu Hui's commentary that every approximation is a little too small or a little too large.
```
