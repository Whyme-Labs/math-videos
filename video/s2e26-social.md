**Description**

```
In 1873 du Bois-Reymond built a continuous function whose Fourier series blows up at a single point, out of layer after layer of hand-tuned wiggles. In 1927 Banach and Steinhaus proved the same thing in a few lines without ever writing the bad function down. Their secret was completeness.

Season 2, episode 26 of Whyme Labs' math series. A family of maps can be bounded at every point and still have norms that grow without limit: bumps that fade at each x while their peaks climb like n, or fₙ(x) = n·xₙ on finite sequences, a space with holes. Baire's theorem says a complete space can't be covered by countably many thin sets: nested discs dodge one thin set after another, their centres form a Cauchy sequence, and the point they land on avoids them all. In ℚ that point is a hole. From Baire come the big three. Uniform boundedness turns pointwise bounds into one bound, ‖T‖ ≤ 2N/r. The open mapping theorem makes a bounded map onto Y open up balls, so a bounded bijection has a bounded inverse. The closed graph theorem makes a linear map with a closed graph bounded, and d/dx on C¹ with the sup norm shows why completeness is needed. The showcase: the Dirichlet kernel's area grows like (4/π²) ln n, so some continuous function's Fourier series diverges at 0, and in fact most of them do. Last, no Banach space has a countable Hamel basis.
```
