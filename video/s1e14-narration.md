## Cold open: the letter to Jena

1. Jena, June 1902. For twenty years Gottlob Frege has been building arithmetic out of pure logic. Volume two is at the printer.
2. A letter arrives from England. A young man named Bertrand Russell admires the work, and has one question.
3. Take the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. Is it a member of itself?
4. If it is, then it isn't. If it isn't, then it is.
5. Frege writes back within a week. The ground he built on has shaken: arithmetic totters.

## Frege's dream: 2 is every pair

1. Frege's dream: no axioms about numbers at all. Only logic, plus the idea from episode eleven: matching.
2. Put one cup on every saucer. If nothing is left over, there are as many cups as saucers, and nobody counted.
3. So the number 2 is the collection of every pair there is: two cups, two shoes, two stars, all at once.

## The crack: the bag R

1. To build that, Frege used one rule that sounds like plain logic: every property makes a set.
2. Most sets are not members of themselves. A bag of cups is not a cup. So gather every bag like that into one bag, R.
3. Is R in R? If it is, it breaks its own rule. If it isn't, it belongs inside. The rule makes R, and R can't exist.

## Russell's repair: floors

1. Russell's repair: give everything a floor. Things on floor 0, sets of things on floor 1, sets of those on floor 2.
2. A set may only hold things from the floor just below it. So the question “is R in R?” isn't false any more. It can't even be asked.
3. The price: there's a 2 on every floor, and to have enough things, Russell had to assume the world holds infinitely many.
