## Cold open: Stettin, 1844

1. Stettin, 1844. Hermann Grassmann, a schoolteacher, publishes a book about adding things and stretching them.
2. Arrows, forces, points, anything at all: if you can add two of them and stretch one by a number, his rules apply.
3. Almost nobody reads it. Möbius can't get through it. Grassmann never gets a university post, and later turns to Sanskrit.
4. Forty-four years on, Peano boils Grassmann's rules down to a short list. Today we call it a vector space.

## Title

1. Season two, episode one. Anything you can add and scale.

## Four things that add

1. Four things that look unrelated: an arrow, a polynomial, a sound, and the waves that solve y double-prime plus y equals zero.
2. Each can be added to another of its kind and stretched by a number. Two arrows tip to tail. Two sounds at once. Two solutions make another solution.

## The rules, and the scalars

1. Keep only those two moves, and a few rules: order doesn't matter, there's a zero, everything has an opposite, stretching spreads over adding.
2. The numbers you stretch by can come from any field from last season: the reals, the complex numbers, even the two-element clock where 1 plus 1 is 0.

## The stamp takes, and bounces

1. Now stamp it on things. Bit strings under XOR: a space with exactly eight vectors. Every function from the reals to the reals. Lists that never end.
2. And watch it bounce. The positive numbers: times minus one and you fall out. A sphere: add two points and you leave it. A line that misses the origin has no zero.

## Payoff: superposition, and zooming in

1. Why it matters: for a linear equation, any mix of solutions is a solution. That's why two sounds can cross a room and come out untouched.
2. And when the world isn't linear, zoom in far enough and it looks linear. That's what a derivative is. Linear algebra is the first draft of everything.
