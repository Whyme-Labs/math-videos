## Cold open: Zurich, 1912

1. Zurich, 1912. Albert Einstein is stuck on gravity, and he turns to his old classmate Marcel Grossmann: help me, or I'll go crazy.
2. Grossmann, a geometer, finds the tool in a memoir by Ricci and Levi-Civita: grids of numbers that change by strict rules when the coordinates change.
3. Physicists still define them like this: a tensor is something that transforms like a tensor. It sounds like a joke.
4. Today we define a tensor by what it does, and every one of those rules falls out on its own.

## The ⊗ funnel

1. A bilinear machine eats two vectors. Put a funnel in front: every pair v, w goes in and comes out as one object, v ⊗ w.
2. Behind the funnel, the two-input machine becomes an ordinary linear one. That's the tensor product's whole job.

## Basis and dimension

1. If V has two basis arrows and W has three, the six products eᵢ ⊗ fⱼ are a basis. Two times three: six dimensions.
2. Putting spaces side by side adds dimensions: two plus three. Tensoring multiplies them: a rectangle instead of a row.

## Pure and entangled

1. A pure tensor is a column times a row: a grid of rank one. Most grids aren't pure. The identity can't be split into one column times one row.
2. In quantum mechanics the tensors that won't split are entangled states: two particles described by one object, not two.

## Payoff: the legs decide the rules

1. Now the rules. Draw a tensor with legs: vector legs and covector legs. Change the basis, and each vector leg picks up P inverse, each covector leg P.
2. A map has one of each: P inverse A P. A form has two covector legs: P transpose B P. Nobody chose these rules. The legs did.
