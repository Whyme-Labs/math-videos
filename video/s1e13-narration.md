## Cold open: the runner after all of them

1. A race with infinitely many runners. Runner 0 crosses the line, then runner 1, runner 2, each one a little quicker than the last.
2. Every runner gets a number, and the announcer never runs out of them.
3. Then, after all of them, one more runner crosses the line. What is her number?
4. It isn't any whole number. Every whole number is already taken by someone ahead of her.
5. There are no more runners than before, but now there is a last one. We need numbers for positions, not just for sizes.

## Title

1. Season one, episode thirteen. Counting past infinity.

## ω and beyond

1. Back in episode four, each number was the set of all the numbers before it. Nothing says we have to stop at the finite ones.
2. Collect every whole number, and you get the first position after all of them: omega. Then omega plus 1, omega plus 2.
3. After all of those, omega times 2. Then omega squared, omega to the omega, and a tower of omegas called epsilon nought.
4. Each one is the set of all the positions before it. These are the ordinals.

## 1 + ω and ω + 1

1. Order matters now. Put one extra runner in front of omega, and everyone just shifts back one: one plus omega is still omega.
2. Put her at the end, and there is a last runner, which omega never had. Omega plus one is a new number.

## Every way down is finite

1. One rule holds all the way up: you can never walk down forever. Every staircase down from any ordinal ends after finitely many steps.
2. Even from omega, your first step down lands on some whole number, and from there the steps run out.

## Goodstein's game

1. Now a game. Take 4, which is 2 to the 2. Swap every 2 for a 3 and subtract one: 26. Then 41, 60, 83, 109.
2. Start at 19 instead, and by the third step the number has over 150 digits. Surely it never comes back down?
3. Swap the base for omega. Swapping changes nothing, and every subtract-one is a step down. So every game ends at 0.
4. In 1982, Kirby and Paris proved that Peano's axioms from episode two can't prove this, though it's only about whole numbers.
