## Arrows become stacks

1. An inner product turns every arrow v into a stack: measure any arrow by its overlap with v. The sheets stand perpendicular to v.
2. And every stack comes from exactly one arrow. That's the Riesz theorem: once you've chosen a ruler, arrows and stacks match one to one.

## The stack stays, the arrow moves

1. Change the inner product and the stack stays put, but the arrow that stands for it swings to the new perpendicular.
2. The stack was the real thing all along. The arrow is the stack, seen through your choice of ruler.

## The gradient needs a ruler

1. Now the gradient. The derivative df is a stack: it counts contours crossed. To turn it into an arrow you need a ruler.
2. With the ordinary dot product you get the textbook gradient. With another ruler, another arrow. Steepest is a choice; how much you climb is not.

## The adjoint

1. And the transpose finally makes sense. Pull a measurement back through a map, then turn it into an arrow again. That round trip is the adjoint.
2. With the dot product the adjoint is the transpose. Change the ruler and it's a different matrix: symmetric only means something once you've chosen how to measure.
