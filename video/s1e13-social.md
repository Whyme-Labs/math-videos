```
A race with infinitely many runners. Runner 0 crosses the line, then runner 1, runner 2, each a little quicker than the last, and the announcer never runs out of numbers. Then, after all of them, one more runner crosses. What is her number? Every whole number is already taken.

Episode 13 of "What Is a Number?". Numbers can count positions, not just sizes. Collect every whole number and you get omega, the first position after all of them; then omega + 1, omega times 2, omega squared, omega to the omega, and epsilon nought. Order matters: one runner in front of omega changes nothing (1 + ω = ω), but one at the end is new (ω + 1). And every staircase down the ordinals is finite. That fact wins Goodstein's game, whose numbers explode past 150 digits and still come back to 0, and Kirby and Paris proved in 1982 that Peano's axioms can't prove it.
```

## LinkedIn (Whyme Labs page, English)

```
Episode 13 of our math series: counting past infinity.

It opens with a race. Infinitely many runners finish, each quicker than the last, and then one more crosses after all of them. No whole number is free for her, so we need a new kind of number: one for positions, not sizes.

Those are Cantor's ordinals: omega, omega + 1, omega squared, epsilon nought. The surprising part is how useful the one rule about them is: any staircase down the ordinals ends after finitely many steps. That rule settles Goodstein's game, a puzzle about ordinary whole numbers whose values blow up past 150 digits before falling back to zero, and which Kirby and Paris showed in 1982 can't be proved from the standard axioms of arithmetic.

The Chinese edition adds a line from the Zhuangzi about a beginning before every beginning, the endless regress that ordinals rule out.
```
