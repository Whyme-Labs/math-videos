## Cold open: you forgot + C

1. An exam hall. A student finds the antiderivative of 2x, writes x squared, and the teacher circles it in red: you forgot plus C.
2. Because x squared, x squared plus 7 and x squared minus pi all have the same derivative. The answer is a whole family of curves.
3. So stop fighting it. Declare the whole family to be one object: glue it into a single point.
4. Season one built the integers, the fractions and clock arithmetic by gluing. Today gluing builds new vector spaces.

## Union vs sum

1. Two lines through the origin. Their union isn't a space: add an arrow from each and you land on neither line.
2. The fix is the sum: every arrow of one plus every arrow of the other. Two different lines sum to the whole plane.

## Complements are not unique

1. Split the plane along the x axis and one other line, and every arrow breaks into a piece on each. But which other line?
2. Swing it, and the pieces change. Every choice works, and no choice is the right one.

## The quotient: glue, don't choose

1. So don't choose. Glue together everything that differs by a step along the axis: each horizontal line becomes one point.
2. What's left is a line of points, one for each height. That's the quotient: the plane modulo the axis.
3. Add two points by adding any arrows from their lines. Whichever arrows you pick, the sum lands on the same line.

## Payoff: + C is a point

1. Now the exam. Differentiation can't tell x squared from x squared plus 7: it crushes the constants. They're its kernel.
2. Glue the kernel away and nothing gets crushed. On polynomials modulo constants, differentiation can be undone.
3. Undo it on 2x and you land on the whole family, x squared plus C. The plus C was never sloppiness. It's a point of the quotient.

## Maps form a space

1. One more. Linear maps can be added and scaled too, so the maps themselves form a vector space.
2. Maps from the plane into space are 3 by 2 matrices: six dials, so six dimensions, and each map is a single point.
