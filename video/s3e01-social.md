**Description**

```
Could a number system have exactly six numbers? Not clock arithmetic: mod 6, 2 × 3 = 0 and you can't divide by 2. A real field, with division. Sizes 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 and 9 all work; 6, 10 and 12 never do. The reason is almost cheating: every bigger field is secretly a vector space, and dimensions multiply.

Season 3, episode 1 of Whyme Labs' math series. Counting can't tell ℚ from ℚ(√2): both are countable. But ℚ(√2) has a new direction, √2, and if you only add and scale by rationals it is a 2-dimensional vector space over ℚ. That dimension is the degree [L : K]: ℂ over ℝ has basis 1, i; ℚ(√2) has basis 1, √2, closed even under 1/(a + b√2); a root of an irreducible degree-n polynomial has degree n, with basis 1, α, …, αⁿ⁻¹. The tower law [M : K] = [M : L]·[L : K] is 'a basis of a basis is a basis': 1, √2 times 1, √3 gives 1, √2, √3, √6. A finite field contains some 𝔽ₚ and is a vector space over it, so it has pⁿ elements, and no field has six. Multiplying by α is a linear map: for a + bi its matrix is [a −b; b a], its determinant a² + b² is the norm, and det(AB) = det A det B is the two-squares identity. Compass-and-straightedge steps have degree 1 or 2, so constructible numbers have degree 2ᵏ, and cos 20°, of degree 3, can't be constructed: no trisection of 60°.
```
