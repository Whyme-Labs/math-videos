**Description**

```
Paris, the night of 29 May 1832. Évariste Galois, twenty years old, has a duel at dawn. He spends the night writing to his friend Auguste Chevalier, racing to set down everything he knows about equations, and scribbles in a margin: "Je n'ai pas le temps", I have no time. Inside is the answer to a question nearly three hundred years old: why the fifth degree has no formula.

Episode 20 of "What Is a Number?", the season finale. Swap the two roots of a quadratic and their sum and product don't change; their difference flips sign, but its square doesn't, and one square root brings it back: that's the quadratic formula. Galois's idea: collect every way the roots can trade places without breaking a rational relation. That's the equation's group. A formula climbs down a ladder of subgroups, one root sign per rung, each rung a step where the moves commute; for degrees 2, 3 and 4 the ladder reaches the bottom. With five roots it jams: every 3-cycle is a commutator of two others. x⁵ − 4x + 2 has all 120 shuffles as its group, so no formula of roots can reach its roots. And the season's answer: a number is whatever behaves like one.
```

```
The Season 1 finale of our math series: Galois.

On the night of 29 May 1832, Évariste Galois, twenty years old and facing a duel at dawn, wrote down the idea that explains why polynomial equations of degree five have no general formula. He didn't look for a cleverer formula. He looked at how the roots can be shuffled without anyone noticing.

That group of shuffles decides everything: a formula in roots exists exactly when the group can be broken down in steps whose moves commute. For degrees 2, 3 and 4 it can; for five roots it can't.

It's also the season's answer to "what is a number?": what matters is how things behave, not what they're made of. Season 2 starts next, with vector spaces.
```
