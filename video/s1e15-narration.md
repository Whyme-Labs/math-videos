## Cold open: Königsberg, 1930

1. Königsberg, September 1930. At a round table on the foundations of mathematics, a quiet 24-year-old named Kurt Gödel speaks up.
2. Almost in passing, he says that arithmetic has true statements its axioms can never prove.
3. Hardly anyone reacts. Only John von Neumann seeks him out afterwards.
4. The next day, in the same city, David Hilbert ends a famous speech: we must know, we will know.
5. But Gödel had just shown that for some questions, the rules alone never will.

## Gödel numbering

1. Gödel's first trick: turn every formula into a number. Give each symbol a code and stack them on primes: 0 = 0 becomes 2 × 27 × 5, which is 270.
2. A proof is a list of formulas, so it's a number too. Now arithmetic can talk about its own proofs.

## I am not provable

1. His second trick is the diagonal from episode twelve. It builds a sentence that says: I am not provable.
2. If the axioms proved it, they'd prove something false. So they can't, and that is exactly what it says. It's true.
3. Add it as a new axiom, and the same trick builds a new one. No consistent rulebook for arithmetic catches every truth.
4. And no such rulebook can prove that it never contradicts itself.

## The continuum hypothesis

1. Back to episode twelve: between the whole numbers and the real line, is there a size in between? Cantor guessed no.
2. In 1940 Gödel showed the standard axioms can't disprove that guess. In 1963 Paul Cohen showed they can't prove it either.
3. The question is perfectly precise, and the axioms simply don't say.

## The axiom of choice

1. One more, the axiom of choice. Infinitely many pairs of shoes? Take every left shoe. Pairs of socks? There's no rule, so you need the axiom.
2. Accept it, and a ball can be cut into five pieces and put back together as two balls the same size as the first.
