**Description**

```
Toss a box in the air with a spin and it wobbles. Spin it about one of three special axes and it turns cleanly. Every rigid object has three, and they are always at right angles. The tilted ellipse 5x² + 4xy + 2y² = 1 has the same secret: its long and short axes cross at 90°. Both come from a symmetric matrix.

Season 2, episode 18 of Whyme Labs' math series. Ordinary eigenbases can fail: a rotation has no eigenvectors, a shear only one, and some eigenbases are almost parallel. If a matrix has perpendicular unit eigenvectors, flipping it across its diagonal changes nothing: symmetry is necessary. The spectral theorem says it's enough. The lemmas are worked as moving formulas: moving A across forces λ⟨u, v⟩ = μ⟨u, v⟩, so eigenvectors for different λ are perpendicular, and every λ is real. In 3D, a symmetric map sends the plane perpendicular to an eigen-arrow into itself while a shear tips it out, and the proof peels eigen-axes off an ellipsoid one at a time. The ellipse turns to its axes and becomes 6X² + Y² = 1; the Hessian of x³ − 3xy + y³ reads a saddle and a bowl off a real surface; and A becomes a sum of stretched shadows, 6P₁ + P₂, which gives √A and quantum measurement.
```
