**Description**

```
Jena, June 1902. For twenty years Gottlob Frege has been building arithmetic out of pure logic, and volume two is at the printer. Then a polite letter arrives from a young Bertrand Russell with one question: take the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. Is it a member of itself?

Episode 14 of "What Is a Number?". Frege's dream was to need no axioms about numbers at all: two collections have the same number when they can be matched, and 2 is the collection of every pair there is. One innocent-looking rule (every property makes a set) lets you build Russell's set R, and R can't exist. Russell's repair was to give everything a floor, the theory of types, at the price of a 2 on every floor and an assumption that the world holds infinitely many things. With Whitehead he wrote it all out in Principia Mathematica, where 1 + 1 = 2 arrives in volume two. And his floors are still here, in every type checker and proof assistant.

## LinkedIn (Whyme Labs page, English)

```
Episode 14 of our math series: Russell's dream.

In June 1902 Gottlob Frege had spent twenty years building arithmetic out of pure logic. Then a letter from Bertrand Russell showed, in a few lines, that one of Frege's basic rules ("every property defines a set") leads to a contradiction: the set of all sets that are not members of themselves.

Frege's reply is one of the most honest letters in science. Russell spent the next decade on a repair, the theory of types, and with Whitehead wrote Principia Mathematica, where every step is explicit and checkable (1 + 1 = 2 arrives in volume two).

That repair never went away. Type systems in programming languages and proof assistants like Lean are its direct descendants.
