## Loss one: the shift

1. Loss one. For a square matrix, one-to-one means onto.
2. Cut the shift down to n boxes: the last number falls off.
3. Grow n. The end runs away. Nothing falls off any more.
4. Shift right, then left: back where you started. LS = I.
5. Left, then right: the first box is lost. SL ≠ I.

## Loss two: a wiggle whose slope explodes

1. Loss two. Every matrix has a biggest stretch, σ₁.
2. Now differentiate f = ε sin(x/ε²).
3. As ε shrinks, the graph flattens onto the axis.
4. Its slope grows like 1/ε, without limit.
5. A linear map turns tiny into huge. No σ₁ exists.

## Loss three: finite sums never finish

1. Loss three. Every vector, a finite sum of basis vectors.
2. Take (1, ½, ⅓, ¼, …). Its squares add up to π²/6.
3. Finite sums of e₁, e₂, … fill it in from the left.
4. Stop at 10: still 0.31 away. At 1000: 0.03.
5. They never finish. We need infinite sums, so limits.

## Loss four: the ball isn't compact

1. Loss four. On a sphere in 3D, points always bunch up.
2. In ℓ², the axes e₁, e₂, e₃, … all have length 1.
3. Every two are √2 apart. None ever get closer.
4. No subsequence settles down. The ball isn't compact.
5. So maximising on the sphere can fail.
6. Here 1 − 1/k creeps towards 1 and never gets there.

## Loss five: no eigenvectors

1. Loss five. Over ℂ, every matrix has an eigenvector.
2. Multiply each function by x. Suppose x·f = λf.
3. Then f is 0 except at λ, so by continuity f = 0.
4. Sampled at n points, it's diagonal. Eigenvectors: spikes.
5. As n grows the spikes thin to nothing. No eigenvectors.
6. Tents of width 1/n come close: they miss by 1/(4n).
