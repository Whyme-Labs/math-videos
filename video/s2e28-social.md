**Description**

```
In the plane, the closest point on a line is easy: drop a perpendicular. Now take every square-integrable function on [0, 1], sound clips, temperature curves, an electron's wave, and ask for the line a + b·x closest to x². Is there one, with the error perpendicular? Yes, as long as you have both angles and completeness.

Season 2, episode 28 of Whyme Labs' math series. An inner product alone isn't enough: among finite sequences, the closest point to e₁ on the plane ⟨x, y⟩ = 0 needs every entry, and without an inner product, balls get corners and closest points split. A Hilbert space has both. The projection theorem follows from the parallelogram law: points closing in on the best distance are Cauchy, completeness gives the limit, and for a subspace the error is perpendicular, so H = M ⊕ M⊥. Riesz representation: every continuous functional is ⟨x, y⟩ for one y, found by dropping a perpendicular onto the kernel; H is its own dual. Orthonormal bases replace finite sums with infinite ones: the best line to x² is x − 1/6 with error² 1/180, Bessel says the pieces never overshoot, and Parseval makes it an equality. Every separable Hilbert space is ℓ², so L²[0, 1], L²(ℝ) and a quantum state space are the same space, and f(t) = t with Parseval gives Σ 1/n² = π²/6.
```
