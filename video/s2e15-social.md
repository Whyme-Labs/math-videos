**Description**

```
Berlin, 1932. John von Neumann publishes the mathematics of quantum mechanics: a particle's state is a vector with complex entries, and lengths give probabilities. Try the dot product on (1, i): 1·1 + i·i = 0. A nonzero vector with length zero. And (1, 2i) comes out at −3.

Season 2, episode 15 of Whyme Labs' math series. A complex number times itself spins twice as fast and its sign wanders; a complex number times its conjugate doesn't spin at all, it's the length squared. So the complex inner product conjugates one slot, and (1, i) gets squared length 1 + 1 = 2. Maps that keep complex lengths are unitary: in the right basis they turn each coordinate round its own circle, and whatever basis you use, their eigenvalues stay on the unit circle. Schur (1909): every complex map is triangular in some orthonormal basis; if it commutes with its adjoint the triangle empties and it's diagonal, with real eigenvalues for Hermitian maps and eigenvalues on the circle for unitary ones. The payoff: the discrete Fourier transform is unitary, so energy is the same in time and frequency, and running it backwards is just the conjugate transpose.
```
