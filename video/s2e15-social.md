**Description**

```
Berlin, 1932. John von Neumann publishes the mathematics of quantum mechanics: a particle's state is a vector with complex entries, and lengths give probabilities. Try the dot product on (1, i): 1·1 + i·i = 0. A nonzero vector with length zero. And (1, 2i) comes out at −3.

Season 2, episode 15 of Whyme Labs' math series. Worked on screen: 1·1 + i·i collapses to 0 while a length gauge falls with it, and (1, 2i) drives it to −3. No cleverer bilinear formula can help: turn any arrow by i and both slots pull out an i, so its 'length²' flips sign. The fix is forced: z·z spins twice as fast while z̄·z sits still at |z|². Conjugate the second slot and (1, i) has length² 2, exactly what ℂ² unfolded into ℝ⁴ says. Unitary maps keep these lengths: in the right basis they turn each coordinate round its own circle, and with entries drawn as arrows you can watch a change of basis fill the matrix while the turning stays the same. A three-line proof puts every unitary eigenvalue on the unit circle, and shows the bar doing the work. Then Schur's staircase of zeros, the spectral theorem, matrix land (real line, unit circle, positive ray), and the payoff: the DFT, divided by √n, is unitary, so energy is the same in time and frequency and the inverse is the conjugate transpose.
```
