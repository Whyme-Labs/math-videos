## Title

1. Season one, episode six. Subtraction and division.

## Cold open

1. What is three minus five? Count down from three and you hit zero after three steps, then fall off.
2. For centuries the fix was to invent a symbol, minus two, plus rules like minus times minus is plus.
3. We won't invent anything. We'll build minus two from the pair three, five, which already exists.
4. Then we'll do the same for three divided by two.

## The integers as diagonals of pairs

1. Write three minus five as the pair three, five. It's just two natural numbers.
2. Zero minus two, one minus three and two minus four should all give the same answer.
3. So call a, b and c, d the same when a plus d equals b plus c. That only needs addition.
4. Each diagonal line is one blob. The whole line is the integer minus two.
5. Collapse every diagonal to a point, and the points line up into the integers.

## Adding pairs; opposites

1. To add, add the pairs part by part. Minus five plus four, as two, seven plus five, one, gives seven, eight.
2. Seven, eight sits on the line of minus one. Other pairs for minus five and four land on the same line.
3. And three, five plus five, three is eight, eight, which is zero. Every integer has an opposite now.

## Why minus times minus is plus

1. Multiplication has a pair formula too. Minus one times minus one comes out as one.
2. That's not a convention. Keep the distributive law, and zero equals minus one plus minus one times minus one.
3. So minus one times minus one has to be one. Any system with negatives and the distributive law agrees.

## The rationals as lines through the centre

1. Division gets the same treatment. Three divided by two has no integer answer, so take the pair three, two.
2. Pairs p, q and r, s match when p times s equals q times r. Each blob is a line through the center.
3. The row with zero on the bottom stays out. Let it in, and zero over zero glues every line into one.
4. Now every fraction except zero has an inverse: two thirds times three halves is one.

## The mediant trap

1. Here's a tempting way to add fractions: add the tops, and add the bottoms.
2. One half plus one third gives two fifths. Write one half as two quarters, and you get three sevenths.
3. Two fifths isn't three sevenths, so this rule isn't well defined. It works on pairs, not on numbers.

## The tower and what we lost

1. The tower gets two new floors. The integers form a ring: you can add, subtract and multiply.
2. The rationals form a field: you can also divide by anything except zero.
3. Each floor cost something. The integers have no smallest number, and no fraction comes right after zero.
