## Measure after mapping

1. A map T carries arrows forward. Measurements travel the other way: to measure an arrow here, map it first, then measure over there.
2. That's the dual map, T star. It pulls a stack of sheets back through T, and the arrow crosses the same number of sheets on both sides.

## The matrix of T* is the transpose

1. Write T star in the dual bases and its matrix is the transpose. Flipping the grid is pulling measurements back.
2. And the order reverses: pulling back through A then B undoes the order of B then A. That's why the transpose of AB is B transpose A transpose.

## Row rank = column rank

1. Here's a 2 by 7 matrix. Its seven columns are arrows in a plane, and only two of them are independent.
2. Its two rows are measurements on a seven-dimensional space, also two independent. Pull back and count: rows and columns always agree.

## Vectors measure measurements

1. Now turn it around. A vector can measure measurements: hand it a covector and it reports the covector's value on itself.
2. So every vector is a point of the double dual. No basis was chosen, and in finite dimensions nothing is missed.

## Payoff: natural vs chosen

1. Matching vectors with covectors is different. Pick a basis and each arrow gets a partner. Change the basis and the partners change.
2. The match with the double dual never moves, whatever basis you pick. That's what natural means, and why category theory exists.
