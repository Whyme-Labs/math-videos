## Cold open: Paris, 1795

1. Paris, 1795. A new law makes the gram the unit of weight, and a grocer has to rewrite every number in the shop.
2. A basket: 2 kilos of apples, 1 of flour, half a kilo of sugar. In grams, those numbers grow a thousand times.
3. The prices per gram shrink a thousand times. And the bill doesn't move: 10 francs 50.
4. Both are lists of three numbers, yet they change in opposite ways. They can't be the same kind of thing.

## Title

## A covector measures

1. The basket is a vector. The price list is a machine: feed it a basket, it hands back a number, and it respects adding and scaling.
2. Draw it as a stack of evenly spaced sheets. Its reading is the number of sheets an arrow crosses, from tail to tip.

## Bigger means denser

1. Double an arrow and it gets longer. Double a covector and its sheets crowd in, twice as close.
2. That's the grocer, drawn: stretch the units, the arrows stretch, the sheets spread out, and the count stays put.

## The dual basis

1. Pick a basis. Its dual basis is a pair of coordinate readers: one reads off the first coordinate, one the second.
2. The first reader's sheets run parallel to the second arrow. Swing the second arrow and the first reader changes, though the first arrow never moved.

## Payoff: the gradient is a covector

1. Now the payoff. Zoom in on a contour map of a hill until the contour lines turn straight, parallel and evenly spaced.
2. That stack is the derivative, df. How far a step climbs is how many contours it crosses. The gradient was a covector all along.
3. The familiar gradient arrow only appears once you decide what perpendicular means. The stack needs no such choice.
