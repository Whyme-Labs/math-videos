## Title

1. Season one, episode seven. The holes in the rationals.

## Cold open

1. Draw a square with sides of one. The diagonal squared is one plus one, which is two.
2. Can a fine enough ruler measure both the side and the diagonal? Halves, tenths, hundredths: the diagonal always falls between two marks.
3. Greek mathematicians proved that no ruler ever works. A much later legend says the man who told was drowned at sea.
4. Fractions seem to fill the line, and yet it's full of holes. Today we find them.

## Fractions are dense

1. First, the case for fractions: between any two of them sits their average, another fraction.
2. Repeat, and the gap fills up. Between any two fractions there are infinitely many more.

## Root two is irrational

1. Suppose a over b squares to two, with b as small as possible. Then a squared equals two b squared.
2. So a squared is even, which makes a even. Write a as two c, and b squared equals two c squared.
3. So b is even too, and c over d is the same fraction with a smaller bottom. That breaks our choice of b.
4. No fraction squares to two. The diagonal has no length in the rationals.

## Zooming in on the hole

1. Zoom in near 1.414. At every level the line looks solid, packed with fractions.
2. Now look closer: the fractions are dust, and at the square root of two there is a gap.
3. Zoom back out and the gaps are everywhere: root three, root five, and infinitely many more between any two fractions.

## No least upper bound

1. Take every fraction whose square is less than two. All of them are below 1.5, so the set has upper bounds.
2. Is there a least upper bound? From any fraction that's too small, one rule gives a bigger one that's still too small.
3. From any fraction that's too big, the same rule gives a smaller one that's still too big.
4. So the two sides close in forever with nothing between them. Some sets of rationals have no least upper bound.

## A sequence with nowhere to land

1. Another view: average x with two over x, and repeat. One, three halves, seventeen twelfths, 577 over 408.
2. The terms crowd together. After four steps, they agree to within a few millionths.
3. A sequence like that ought to have a limit, but the limit would square to two. It has nowhere to land.

## The tower

1. So the rationals have holes everywhere, and adding root two alone won't fix them.
2. The next floor needs a new idea: limits.
