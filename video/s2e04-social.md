**Description**

```
Dublin, 16 October 1843. William Rowan Hamilton is walking along the Royal Canal. For years he has tried to multiply triples of numbers the way complex numbers multiply pairs. At Broom Bridge it comes to him: use four numbers, and let i times j differ from j times i. He carves i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1 into the stone.

Season 2, episode 4 of Whyme Labs' math series. Write down where a map sends each basis arrow and those images become the columns: that grid is the matrix, and mixing the columns by an arrow's coordinates does the map in numbers. Do one map, then another, follow a basis arrow through both, and row-times-column falls out: it isn't a convention. Turn a square then flatten it and you get a short flat shadow; flatten then turn and you get a tall one, so AB ≠ BA, like socks and shoes. And the payoff: change the basis and the same map casts a different matrix. The tilted [[2, 1], [1, 2]] is, seen along the diagonals, just stretch by 3 and stretch by 1.
```

**Thread** (4 posts):

2. `Write where a map sends each basis vector. Those are the columns. Mix the columns by a vector's coordinates and you've applied the map.`
3. `Apply two maps in a row and follow a basis vector through both: each entry is a row of the second against a column of the first. Row × column is forced, not chosen.`
