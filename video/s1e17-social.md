**Description**

```
Three friends on a video call, three different toys: a square tile turning a quarter turn, a four-hour clock ticking forward, and the number i multiplying 1 → i → −1 → −i. Hide the labels and you can't tell them apart. The pictures are different; the pattern is the same.

Episode 17 of "What Is a Number?". Two groups are the same (isomorphic) when one can be relabeled as the other with every move still matching; same size isn't enough, as a rectangle's four moves show. A homomorphism is a map that respects the moves but may forget: sending every integer to its hour on a four-hour clock forgets exactly the multiples of 4, its kernel. The kernel slices the integers into equal lanes (cosets), which gives Lagrange's theorem, and squashing each lane to a point gives back the clock: the first isomorphism theorem, every homomorphism is a collapse followed by a relabeling.

## LinkedIn (Whyme Labs page, English)

```
Episode 17 of our math series: homomorphisms.

It starts on a video call: three friends with a square tile, a four-hour clock and the complex number i. Each makes the same kind of move, and with the labels hidden their screens are indistinguishable. That's what mathematicians mean by "the same": an isomorphism, a relabeling that preserves every move.

Then the more useful idea: maps that preserve structure but forget detail. The map from the integers to a four-hour clock forgets exactly the multiples of 4, its kernel. That one observation gives cosets, Lagrange's theorem, and the first isomorphism theorem, which says every structure-preserving map is a collapse followed by a relabeling. The same pattern returns later as rank–nullity in linear algebra.

The Chinese edition adds the classic remainder problem from the Sunzi Suanjing: three clocks that each forget almost everything, and together pin down 23.
