## Cold open: Paris, 1847

1. Paris, March 1847. Gabriel Lamé tells the Academy of Sciences he has proved Fermat's Last Theorem.
2. His trick: let some new numbers into arithmetic, factor both sides of the equation, and compare the primes.
3. Joseph Liouville stands up. Your proof assumes these new numbers split into primes in only one way. Do they?
4. That May, a letter from Ernst Kummer is read out: they don't. He had shown it three years before.
5. To see what broke, we need the rules for every world where you can add and multiply.

## Title

1. Season one, episode eighteen. Rings.

## The ring rules

1. A ring is a world where you can add, subtract and multiply, and multiplying spreads over adding: a times b plus c is ab plus ac.
2. That one rule forces minus one times minus one to be one. It isn't a convention: once multiplying spreads over adding, there's no other choice.

## 2 × 3 = 0, and partners

1. On a six-hour clock, 2 times 3 is 0. Two things that aren't nothing multiply to nothing.
2. On a seven-hour clock, that never happens. Seven is prime, so every number but 0 has a partner that multiplies it to 1.

## Glue in x² + 1 = 0

1. Now take polynomials and add one rule: x squared plus 1 is 0. Every time x squared appears, swap it for minus 1.
2. What's left is a plus b x, and it multiplies exactly like the complex numbers from episode ten. x has become i.

## Two factorizations of 6

1. Lamé's trouble, in a smaller world: allow root minus five. Then 6 is 2 times 3, and also 1 plus root minus five, times 1 minus root minus five.
2. None of those four can be split any further. One number, two different factorizations into primes.
3. Kummer and Dedekind's repair: factor the sets of multiples, called ideals, instead of the numbers. Then factorization is unique again.
