**Description**

```
A row of spikes on [0, 1], each taller and thinner: the n-th is n tall and 1/n² wide. Do they shrink to zero? The accountant measures area and says yes. The engineer measures energy and says it stays at ⅓. The inspector measures the peak and says it explodes. Same functions, three verdicts. In the plane this can't happen.

Season 2, episode 21 of Whyme Labs' math series. A norm is a length with three rules, and it's determined by its unit ball: the triangle rule holds exactly when the ball is convex, and a star-shaped ball breaks it. On sequences the rules give ℓ¹ ⊂ ℓ² ⊂ ℓ∞. Two norms agree when each is at most a constant times the other; for the spikes, peak ÷ area = 2n², so no constant works. In the plane a sandwich of two circles proves every norm agrees with ordinary length, and the one step that needs finite dimension is that the circle is compact. Good linear maps respect small: ‖Tx‖ ≤ C‖x‖, bounded is the same as continuous, and differentiation fails until you charge for slope too. One matrix gets three operator norms, 6, 5.46 and 7. Riesz's lemma shows the unit ball is compact only in finite dimensions, and ramps steepening toward a step show the holes in continuous functions under the area norm.
```
