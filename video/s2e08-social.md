**Description**

```
Ann Arbor, 1941. Samuel Eilenberg sits in on Saunders Mac Lane's lectures and the two start working together. Mathematicians kept calling some isomorphisms "natural" and others not, and nobody could say what the word meant. In 1945 the two wrote down the answer, and it took a new branch of mathematics: category theory. Their first example: a space is naturally the same as its double dual, but not as its dual.

Season 2, episode 8 of Whyme Labs' math series. A map T carries arrows forward; measurements travel back. The dual map T* pulls a stack of sheets back through T, and the arrow crosses the same number of sheets on both sides. In dual bases its matrix is the transpose, and pulling back reverses order, which is why (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ. Counting dimensions on both sides shows that a matrix's rows and columns always have the same rank. Then turn it round: a vector measures measurements, so every vector is a point of the double dual with no basis chosen. Matching vectors to covectors needs a basis and changes when it changes; the match with the double dual never moves. That is what "natural" means.
```

```
The transpose isn't "flip the table". It's measuring after mapping: pull a measurement back through a map and its matrix is the transpose.
```

**Thread** (4 posts):

1. `Why does a matrix always have as many independent rows as independent columns? 🧵`
2. `A map T sends vectors forward. Measurements go backward: T* measures v by mapping it first. In dual bases, T*'s matrix is the transpose.`
3. `The measurements that see nothing of T's image are the kernel of T*. Count dimensions on both sides and the images match: row rank = column rank.`
4. `Bonus: V ≅ V** needs no choice of basis; V ≅ V* does. Eilenberg and Mac Lane built category theory to say exactly that. <YouTube link>`
