## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson the viewer can:
1. State the group axioms and decide whether a given set with an operation is a group, naming the axiom that fails when it isn't.
2. List the 8 symmetries of a square, compose them, and fill in the Cayley table of the dihedral group D₄.
3. Prove basic consequences of the axioms: uniqueness of the identity and of inverses, the "socks and shoes" rule $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$, and the Latin-square (sudoku) property of a Cayley table.
4. Explain the sense in which ℤ under addition is "the group freely generated by one element," and why that makes counting a group-theoretic idea.

## Cold open
*Narrator:*

"Pick up a square of paper. Without looking, a friend picks it up, does something to it, and puts it back exactly in its outline. How many different things could they have done? You can turn it a quarter, a half, three quarters, or not at all. You can flip it over, along either diagonal or along either midline. That's eight. Eight moves that leave the square looking untouched.

Now look at a clock. It has twelve positions for the hour hand, and 'add five hours' is a move that takes positions to positions.

A Rubik's cube has more moves than that. Its scrambles number about 43 quintillion.

These look like three different worlds: geometry, timekeeping, a toy. But play with them long enough and you notice that the *moves* follow the same rules. You can undo any move. Doing nothing is a move. Doing one move and then another is again a move. And it doesn't matter how you group a long sequence of moves.

So here's the question for today. Is there one kind of structure hiding behind the square, the clock, and the cube? And if there is, what *is* it, if not squares, hours, or colored stickers? This is the moment the series has been building toward: we stop caring what things are made of, and keep only what you can do with them."

## The story

### 1. The naive attempt: symmetries are things you can do
A **symmetry** of a shape is a rigid motion that maps the shape onto itself. Label the corners of a square 1, 2, 3, 4 counterclockwise. A symmetry is determined by where it sends the corners, so we can record it as a permutation.

- $r$: rotate 90° counterclockwise. Corner 1 goes to where 2 was, and so on: $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$.
- $s$: flip across the diagonal through corners 1 and 3. It fixes 1 and 3 and swaps 2 and 4: $s = (2\,4)$.

Why are there exactly 8? Corner 1 can land on any of the 4 corners. Its neighbor, corner 2, must land next to it: 2 choices. After that, rigidity forces everything else. So there are $4 \times 2 = 8$ symmetries:
$$e,\ r,\ r^2,\ r^3,\ s,\ rs,\ r^2 s,\ r^3 s.$$
Here $xy$ means "do $y$ first, then $x$," the same convention as composing functions, $(x \circ y)(p) = x(y(p))$.

The naive approach would study each shape separately: squares in geometry, clocks in arithmetic, cubes in puzzle books. But the same three behaviors keep showing up.

### 2. The crisis: what exactly do these examples share?
Look at several systems side by side.

| System | Elements | Operation | Undo? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetries of the square | 8 motions | do one, then the other | each motion can be reversed |
| Clock (↩ S1E05) | 0, 1, …, 11 | addition mod 12 | $a$ is undone by $12 - a$ |
| ℤ (↩ S1E06) | integers | + | $a$ is undone by $-a$ |
| ℚ \ {0} | nonzero fractions | × | $a$ is undone by $1/a$ |
| Permutations of {1, …, n} | rearrangements | composition | the inverse rearrangement |
| Rubik's cube | reachable scrambles | do one move sequence, then another | play the moves backwards |

And some systems that look similar but behave worse:

- **ℕ under +:** you can't undo "add 3." This was exactly the defect that made us build ℤ in S1E06.
- **ℤ under ×:** you can't undo "multiply by 2" without leaving ℤ.
- **ℤ under −:** "undoing" exists, but $(5 - 3) - 1 = 1$ while $5 - (3 - 1) = 3$. Grouping matters, so long computations become ambiguous.

What's missing is a precise list of the rules that the good examples share and the bad ones break.

### 3. The idea: the group axioms
> **Definition (group).** A **group** is a set $G$ with an operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ (so $a \cdot b \in G$ for all $a, b$: **closure**) such that
> 1. **Associativity:** $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
> 2. **Identity:** there is $e \in G$ with $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for all $a$.
> 3. **Inverses:** for each $a \in G$ there is $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.
>
> The group is **abelian** (commutative) if also $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b$. The **order** $|G|$ is the number of elements. The **order of an element** $g$ is the least $n \ge 1$ with $g^n = e$ (or ∞ if there is none).

The definition says nothing about what the elements *are*. A group is a set together with a way of combining, constrained only by behavior. That is the series thesis at work: "defined by what you can do with it."

Some immediate consequences follow from the axioms alone, so they hold in *every* group at once.

> **Proposition.** In any group:
> 1. The identity is unique.
> 2. Each element has exactly one inverse.
> 3. **Cancellation:** if $ab = ac$ then $b = c$, and if $ba = ca$ then $b = c$.
> 4. **Socks and shoes:** $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1} a^{-1}$.

*Proof.*
1. If $e$ and $e'$ are both identities, then $e = e e' = e'$ (the first equality because $e'$ is an identity, the second because $e$ is).
2. If $b$ and $c$ are both inverses of $a$, then $b = b e = b(ac) = (ba)c = e c = c$.
3. Multiply $ab = ac$ on the left by $a^{-1}$ and use associativity: $(a^{-1}a)b = (a^{-1}a)c$, so $eb = ec$, so $b = c$. The right-hand version is symmetric.
4. Check that $b^{-1}a^{-1}$ works as an inverse of $ab$: $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(bb^{-1})a^{-1} = a e a^{-1} = aa^{-1} = e$, and similarly in the other order. By part 2, it is *the* inverse. ∎

The name of part 4: you put on socks, then shoes; to undo, you take off shoes first, then socks. (Yes, it's the same footwear as Russell's socks in S1E15.) The order reverses. In an abelian group it doesn't matter, but for the square it does, as we'll see.

> **Theorem (Latin square / sudoku property).** In the Cayley table of a finite group (row $a$, column $b$, entry $ab$), every element appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column.

*Proof.* Fix row $a$. If two entries were equal, $ab = ac$, then $b = c$ by cancellation, so they're in the same column. Hence the $|G|$ entries in the row are distinct, and since there are $|G|$ of them, every element appears exactly once. Columns: the same argument with right cancellation. ∎

### 4. A gallery of groups
- **Cyclic groups** $\mathbb{Z}/n$: the clock with $n$ positions, under addition mod n. Abelian, order $n$. ℤ itself is the infinite cyclic group.
- **Dihedral groups** $D_n$: symmetries of a regular n-gon, order $2n$ ($n$ rotations, $n$ reflections). Non-abelian for $n \ge 3$. The square gives $D_4$, of order 8. (Some books write $D_8$ for this group, indexing by order; we index by the number of sides.)
- **Symmetric groups** $S_n$: all permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, order $n!$. Non-abelian for $n \ge 3$. Every finite group lives inside one of these (Cayley's theorem, ↪ S1E17).
- **Multiplicative groups:** $\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$, $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ under ×; the unit circle in ℂ under × (rotations, ↩ S1E10); the units $\{1, 5, 7, 11\}$ of clock arithmetic mod 12 under ×.
- **Matrix groups:** $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, invertible $n \times n$ real matrices under matrix multiplication. Non-abelian for $n \ge 2$.
- **The Rubik's cube group:** all positions reachable from the solved cube. Its order is
$$\frac{8! \cdot 3^8 \cdot 12! \cdot 2^{12}}{12} = 43{,}252{,}003{,}274{,}489{,}856{,}000 = 2^{27}\, 3^{14}\, 5^{3}\, 7^{2}\, 11.$$
  (Corners can be permuted $8!$ ways and twisted $3^8$ ways; edges permuted $12!$ ways and flipped $2^{12}$ ways; but only $1/12$ of these are reachable, because of three hidden constraints: total corner twist, total edge flip, and matching permutation parities.)

**Abelian vs. non-abelian.** In $D_4$:
- $rs$: first flip across the 1–3 diagonal, then rotate. Corner by corner: $1 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3$. So $rs = (1\,2)(3\,4)$, the flip across the line through the midpoints of edges 12 and 34.
- $sr$: first rotate, then flip. $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 2$. So $sr = (1\,4)(2\,3)$.

Different! So $D_4$ is non-abelian. In fact $sr = r^3 s = r^{-1}s$, and this single rule, together with $r^4 = e$ and $s^2 = e$, determines the whole multiplication table.

### 5. Payoff: ℤ is the group freely generated by one element
Go back to Peano (↩ S1E02): ℕ is generated by 0 and "next." ℤ is generated by one element, 1, together with "next" *and* "previous." Group language says this precisely.

> **Theorem (ℤ is free on one generator).** Let $G$ be any group and $g \in G$ any element. There is exactly one function $\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to G$ with $\varphi(1) = g$ and $\varphi(m + n) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$ for all $m, n$. It is $\varphi(n) = g^n$.

Here $g^0 = e$, $g^{n+1} = g^n g$ for $n \ge 0$ (a recursive definition exactly like S1E03's), and $g^{-n} = (g^{-1})^n$.

*Proof sketch.* **Uniqueness:** $\varphi(0) = \varphi(0 + 0) = \varphi(0)\varphi(0)$, and cancelling gives $\varphi(0) = e$. Then $\varphi(n + 1) = \varphi(n)g$ forces $\varphi(n) = g^n$ for $n \ge 0$ by induction, and $\varphi(-n)\varphi(n) = \varphi(0) = e$ forces $\varphi(-n) = (g^n)^{-1} = g^{-n}$. **Existence:** the exponent law $g^{m+n} = g^m g^n$ holds in any group (induction on $n$, then cases for signs). ∎

"Freely generated" means: 1 satisfies *no relation* except those forced by the axioms. In $\mathbb{Z}/12$, the generator satisfies $12 \cdot 1 = 0$, a relation; in ℤ, nothing like that. Every other cyclic group is ℤ with a relation added, which is the quotient construction of S1E17.

So what is counting? Counting is repeatedly applying one generator. "Three" is "do it three times"; "minus three" is "undo it three times." Counting itself is a group-theoretic idea: ℤ is the most general thing you can build from a single invertible move.

The same idea with *two* generators gives the **free group** $F_2$: all reduced words in $a, a^{-1}, b, b^{-1}$, such as $ab^{-1}a a$, multiplied by concatenating and cancelling. It is wildly non-abelian, and two suitable rotations of a sphere generate a copy of it. That is the engine of the Banach–Tarski paradox from S1E15.

### 6. Zoom out: symmetry means what stays fixed
The answer to the cold open: yes, the square, the clock and the cube share one structure, the group. But they are *different* groups: $D_4$ (order 8, non-abelian), $\mathbb{Z}/12$ (order 12, abelian), and the Rubik's group (enormous, non-abelian).

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves something unchanged (thread 5 of the series). The square's symmetries preserve the square; the group of all invertible linear maps preserves linearity (↪ S2E04); Galois will study the transformations of the roots of a polynomial that preserve every algebraic relation among them (↪ S1E20). In each case the group *is* the collection of transformations that keep something fixed.

**Teaser.** The four rotations $\{e, r, r^2, r^3\}$ of the square compose exactly like the four positions of a 4-hour clock, and exactly like $\{1, i, -1, -i\}$ under multiplication in ℂ. Are they "the same group"? What does "the same" even mean for groups? That's the next episode.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: The Cayley table of D₄
Elements, as permutations of corners (corners 1–4 counterclockwise):

| Symmetry | Permutation | Description |
|---|---|---|
| $e$ | identity | do nothing |
| $r$ | $(1\,2\,3\,4)$ | rotate 90° |
| $r^2$ | $(1\,3)(2\,4)$ | rotate 180° |
| $r^3$ | $(1\,4\,3\,2)$ | rotate 270° |
| $s$ | $(2\,4)$ | flip across diagonal 1–3 |
| $rs$ | $(1\,2)(3\,4)$ | flip across the line through the midpoints of edges 12 and 34 |
| $r^2 s$ | $(1\,3)$ | flip across diagonal 2–4 |
| $r^3 s$ | $(1\,4)(2\,3)$ | flip across the line through the midpoints of edges 14 and 23 |

Rules: $r^4 = e$, $s^2 = e$, $sr = r^3 s$. More generally $s r^k = r^{-k} s$. Every product can be reduced to the form $r^i s^j$:

- $r \cdot r^3 s = r^4 s = s$.
- $s \cdot r = r^3 s$.
- $s \cdot rs = (sr)s = r^3 s s = r^3$.
- $rs \cdot rs = r(sr)s = r \cdot r^3 s \cdot s = r^4 s^2 = e$. (Every reflection has order 2.)
- $r^2 s \cdot r^3 s = r^2 (s r^3) s = r^2 \cdot r^{-3} s \cdot s = r^{-1} = r^3$.

The full table (row $a$, column $b$, entry $ab$ = "do $b$, then $a$"), computed by these rules and checked by composing permutations:

| · | e | r | r² | r³ | s | rs | r²s | r³s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **e** | e | r | r² | r³ | s | rs | r²s | r³s |
| **r** | r | r² | r³ | e | rs | r²s | r³s | s |
| **r²** | r² | r³ | e | r | r²s | r³s | s | rs |
| **r³** | r³ | e | r | r² | r³s | s | rs | r²s |
| **s** | s | r³s | r²s | rs | e | r³ | r² | r |
| **rs** | rs | s | r³s | r²s | r | e | r³ | r² |
| **r²s** | r²s | rs | s | r³s | r² | r | e | r³ |
| **r³s** | r³s | r²s | rs | s | r³ | r² | r | e |

Checks: each row and column is a permutation of the 8 elements (the sudoku property). The top-left 4×4 block (rotations) is closed and symmetric across its diagonal, so the rotations alone form an abelian group. The table as a whole is *not* symmetric: row $s$, column $r$ gives $r^3 s$, but row $r$, column $s$ gives $rs$. Element orders: $e$ has order 1; $r, r^3$ have order 4; $r^2$ and all four reflections have order 2.

### Example 2: Which of these are groups?
**(a) $\mathbb{Z}/12$ under addition mod 12.** Closure: sums are reduced mod 12. Associativity: inherited from ℤ, since reducing mod 12 respects addition (↩ S1E05). Identity: 0. Inverses: $a \mapsto 12 - a$ (and $0 \mapsto 0$); e.g. $5 + 7 = 12 \equiv 0$. **A group**, abelian, order 12. The order of the element 8 is 3: $8, 16 \equiv 4, 24 \equiv 0$.

**(b) $\{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ under multiplication mod 12.** Identity 1 exists, but 0 has no inverse, and neither does 2: $2x$ is always even, so $2x \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$ is impossible. **Not a group** (inverses fail).

**(c) $U(12) = \{1, 5, 7, 11\}$ under multiplication mod 12.** Compute all products:
- $5 \cdot 5 = 25 = 2 \cdot 12 + 1 \equiv 1$
- $7 \cdot 7 = 49 = 4 \cdot 12 + 1 \equiv 1$
- $11 \cdot 11 = 121 = 10 \cdot 12 + 1 \equiv 1$
- $5 \cdot 7 = 35 = 2 \cdot 12 + 11 \equiv 11$
- $5 \cdot 11 = 55 = 4 \cdot 12 + 7 \equiv 7$
- $7 \cdot 11 = 77 = 6 \cdot 12 + 5 \equiv 5$

| × | 1 | 5 | 7 | 11 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 1 | 5 | 7 | 11 |
| **5** | 5 | 1 | 11 | 7 |
| **7** | 7 | 11 | 1 | 5 |
| **11** | 11 | 7 | 5 | 1 |

Closed, identity 1, every element is its own inverse, associative (inherited from ℤ). **A group**, abelian, order 4, and every non-identity element has order 2. Note: this is a group of order 4 that is *not* like the 4-hour clock, where the element 1 has order 4. Two different groups of order 4. (↪ S1E17 makes "different" precise.)

**(d) ℤ under subtraction.** Closure holds, but $(5 - 3) - 1 = 1 \neq 3 = 5 - (3 - 1)$. **Not a group** (associativity fails).

**(e) The odd integers under +.** $1 + 1 = 2$ is not odd. **Not a group** (closure fails).

### Example 3: Permutations don't commute
In $S_3$, use cycle notation and compose right to left.

**Compute $(1\,2)(2\,3)$:** apply $(2\,3)$ first, then $(1\,2)$.
- $1 \xrightarrow{(2\,3)} 1 \xrightarrow{(1\,2)} 2$
- $2 \xrightarrow{(2\,3)} 3 \xrightarrow{(1\,2)} 3$
- $3 \xrightarrow{(2\,3)} 2 \xrightarrow{(1\,2)} 1$

So $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$: the product is $(1\,2\,3)$.

**Compute $(2\,3)(1\,2)$:** apply $(1\,2)$ first.
- $1 \to 2 \to 3$
- $3 \to 3 \to 2$
- $2 \to 1 \to 1$

So $1 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1$: the product is $(1\,3\,2)$.

The two orders differ, so $S_3$ is non-abelian. Socks and shoes check: $\big((1\,2)(2\,3)\big)^{-1} = (2\,3)^{-1}(1\,2)^{-1} = (2\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,3\,2)$, and indeed $(1\,2\,3)^{-1} = (1\,3\,2)$. ✓

Element orders in $S_3$: $e$ (order 1), three transpositions (order 2), two 3-cycles (order 3). Since $\mathbb{Z}/6$ has an element of order 6 and $S_3$ has none, these two groups of order 6 are genuinely different.

### Example 4: Using the universal property of ℤ
**(a)** Take $G = \mathbb{Z}/12$, $g = 5$. The unique structure-preserving map is $\varphi(n) = 5n \bmod 12$. Values: $\varphi(0) = 0$, $\varphi(1) = 5$, $\varphi(2) = 10$, $\varphi(3) = 3$, $\varphi(4) = 8$, $\varphi(5) = 1$, …, $\varphi(12) = 0$, and $\varphi(-1) = -5 \equiv 7$. Check one instance of $\varphi(m + n) = \varphi(m) + \varphi(n)$: $\varphi(7) = 35 \bmod 12 = 11$, and $\varphi(3) + \varphi(4) = 3 + 8 = 11$. ✓

**(b)** Take $G = D_4$, $g = r$. Then $\varphi(n) = r^n$, and since $r^4 = e$ the values repeat with period 4: $\varphi(5) = r$, $\varphi(-1) = r^3$. Check: $\varphi(2 + 3) = r^5 = r$ and $\varphi(2)\varphi(3) = r^2 r^3 = r^5 = r$. ✓

**(c)** Take $G = D_4$, $g = s$. Then $\varphi(n) = s^n$, which is $e$ for even $n$ and $s$ for odd $n$. One generator, with any destination we like: that's what "free" means.

## Common misconceptions
- **Misconception:** a group is a set of numbers. **Reality:** a group is a set *with an operation* obeying the axioms. The same set can be a group under one operation and not another ($\mathbb{Z}$ under + vs. ×), and the elements can be motions, permutations or puzzle moves.
- **Misconception:** the operation in a group is always commutative. **Reality:** $D_4$, $S_3$, matrix groups and the Rubik's cube group are non-abelian. Order of operations matters, as with socks and shoes.
- **Misconception:** closure is automatic. **Reality:** it must be checked (odd integers under + fail it). It is often the first thing that goes wrong when you take a subset of a group.
- **Misconception:** $(ab)^{-1} = a^{-1}b^{-1}$. **Reality:** $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$; the two agree only when $a$ and $b$ commute.
- **Misconception:** two groups with the same number of elements are the same group. **Reality:** $\mathbb{Z}/4$ and $U(12)$ both have order 4 but differ (one has an element of order 4, the other doesn't). $\mathbb{Z}/6$ and $S_3$ both have order 6, but one is abelian and the other isn't.

## Historical note
Group theory grew from several roots at once. Lagrange (1770–71) studied how expressions in the roots of a polynomial change when the roots are permuted, and Cauchy developed the calculus of permutations in the 1810s–1840s. Évariste Galois, around 1830, was the first to use the word "group" (*groupe*) for a collection of permutations closed under composition, and to see that the structure of that group decides whether an equation can be solved by radicals (↪ S1E20). Geometry and number theory contributed groups of transformations and of residues (Gauss's *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, 1801, works with what we now call cyclic groups mod n).

The first abstract definition, a set with an operation satisfying axioms and no mention of permutations, is usually credited to Arthur Cayley (1854), though his idea took decades to catch on. Felix Klein's Erlangen Program (1872) proposed that a geometry *is* the study of the invariants of a group of transformations: the same "symmetry means what stays fixed" thread as this episode. The modern axiomatic definition was standard by the early twentieth century.

  - Felix Klein, *Vergleichende Betrachtungen über neuere geometrische Forschungen* (the "Erlangen Program," 1872).
- **Textbooks**
  - Charles C. Pinter, *A Book of Abstract Algebra* (2nd ed., McGraw-Hill 1990; Dover reprint 2010), Chapters 3–5 (groups, elementary properties, subgroups) and Chapter 7 (groups of permutations).
  - David S. Dummit and Richard M. Foote, *Abstract Algebra* (3rd ed., Wiley, 2004), Chapter 1 (introduction to groups, including dihedral and symmetric groups).
  - Michael Artin, *Algebra* (2nd ed., Pearson, 2011), Chapter 2 (groups) and Chapter 6 (symmetry).
  - Joseph A. Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* (Cengage, many editions), early chapters on groups, with the Rubik's cube and dihedral examples.
- **Accessible**
  - Nathan Carter, *Visual Group Theory* (Mathematical Association of America, 2009): Cayley diagrams and tables throughout.
  - David Joyner, *Adventures in Group Theory: Rubik's Cube, Merlin's Machine, and Other Mathematical Toys* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2002; 2nd ed. 2008).
  - Mark Ronan, *Symmetry and the Monster* (Oxford University Press, 2006): a popular history of group theory.
