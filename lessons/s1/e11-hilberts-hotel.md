# S1E11: Hilbert's Hotel: Counting Without Numbers

> **Season / Act:** Season 1, Act III (Infinity and the Foundations Crisis), opening episode · **Target runtime:** 15–20 min ·
> **Prerequisites:** S1E02 (ℕ starts at 0 and is generated by "next"), S1E06 (ℤ and ℚ); outside knowledge: functions, and what "one-to-one" means informally ·
> **Builds toward:** S1E12 (not everything is countable), S1E13 (ordinals: "position in line" vs. "how many"), S1E14 (Frege–Russell: a number *is* a class of matchable collections), S1E17 (isomorphisms), S2E22 (countable sets have measure zero)

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson the viewer can:
1. Define "same size" for arbitrary sets using bijections, and define countable sets.
2. Build explicit bijections ℕ → evens, ℕ → ℤ, and ℕ × ℕ → ℕ (the Cantor pairing function), and invert them.
3. Prove that ℚ is countable, using the Calkin–Wilf tree, and locate a given fraction in the Calkin–Wilf list.
4. Explain why "a proper subset is smaller" fails for infinite sets, and why that is not a contradiction.

## Cold open
*Narrator, in front of a neon sign: "HOTEL INFINITY — ROOMS 0, 1, 2, 3, …"*

There's a hotel with infinitely many rooms, numbered 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on forever. Tonight, every single room is occupied.

A traveler walks in and asks for a room. In any normal hotel, you'd send them away. But the manager here just picks up the intercom: "Attention, guests. Please move from your room *n* to room *n* + 1." Everyone shifts one door down the hall. Room 0 is now empty. The traveler gets the key.

The hotel was full. Nobody left. And now there's a free room.

Then a bus pulls up, with *infinitely many* passengers. The manager doesn't blink: "Everyone in room *n*, move to room 2*n*." Now every guest is in an even room, every odd room is empty, and the infinite busload walks right in.

Then infinitely many *buses* arrive, each with infinitely many passengers.

Hold that thought. Before we can help the manager, we have to answer a question that sounds childish: what does it mean for two collections to have the *same number* of things, when you can't count to the end of either one?

## The story

### 1. The naive attempt: "a part is smaller than the whole"

Euclid listed it among his common notions: *the whole is greater than the part*. For finite collections, it's a theorem. Remove one apple from a basket and you have fewer apples.

So it seems obvious that there are *fewer* even numbers than natural numbers. The evens are a part of ℕ, and a "thin" part at that: half of the numbers are missing.

But look at this:

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | … |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2n$ | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | … |

Every natural number is paired with exactly one even number, and every even number is paired with exactly one natural number. Nobody is left out on either side. That is precisely how a child checks that two piles of sweets are equal without knowing how to count: pair them off.

Galileo noticed exactly this paradox in 1638 with squares instead of evens ($1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots$ can be paired with $1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots$) and concluded that "equal," "greater," and "less" simply do not apply to infinite quantities.

### 2. The crisis: which principle do we give up?

For infinite sets, two intuitions collide:
- **Part–whole principle:** a proper part is smaller.
- **Matching principle:** if two collections can be perfectly paired, they are the same size.

They can't both hold, since the evens are a proper part of ℕ *and* can be perfectly paired with it. Galileo gave up on comparing infinite sets altogether. Cantor, two and a half centuries later, made the opposite choice: keep matching, drop part–whole. That choice turned out to be enormously fruitful, because matching is something we can actually check.

> **Definition (bijection).** A function $f : A \to B$ is a **bijection** if it is
> - **injective** (one-to-one): $f(a) = f(a')$ implies $a = a'$, and
> - **surjective** (onto): every $b \in B$ equals $f(a)$ for some $a \in A$.
>
> Equivalently, $f$ has an inverse function $f^{-1} : B \to A$ with $f^{-1}(f(a)) = a$ and $f(f^{-1}(b)) = b$.

> **Definition (equinumerous; cardinality).** Sets $A$ and $B$ are **equinumerous**, written $|A| = |B|$, if there is a bijection $A \to B$. We write $|A| \le |B|$ if there is an injection $A \to B$.

> **Definition (countable).** A set is **countably infinite** if it is equinumerous with ℕ, and **countable** if it is finite or countably infinite. A bijection $f : \mathbb{N} \to A$ is the same thing as a list $f(0), f(1), f(2), \ldots$ in which every element of $A$ appears exactly once.

Equinumerosity behaves like "sameness" should: it is reflexive (the identity map), symmetric (inverse of a bijection), and transitive (composition of bijections). It is an equivalence relation in the sense of ↩ S1E05, on any given collection of sets.

Notice the shift in perspective. We have not said what "the number of elements" *is*. We have only said when two sets have the same one. Size is defined by what you can *do*, namely match, not by a count. (In ↪ S1E14 Frege and Russell take the next step and *define* a number to be a class of matchable collections.)

The part–whole paradox now becomes a *characterization*:

> **Fact (Dedekind's definition of infinite).** A set is infinite if and only if it is equinumerous with a proper subset of itself. (The "if" direction holds outright; the "only if" direction uses a weak form of the Axiom of Choice, ↪ S1E15.)

The hotel is the picture of this fact: shifting every guest $n \mapsto n + 1$ is a bijection from ℕ onto the proper subset $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$.

### 3. Building bijections: evens, integers, pairs

**Evens.** $f(n) = 2n$ is a bijection $\mathbb{N} \to E = \{0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$, with inverse $f^{-1}(m) = m/2$.

**Integers.** List ℤ by alternating signs: $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, -3, 3, \ldots$. As a formula,
$$f(n) = \begin{cases} n/2 & n \text{ even}, \\ -(n+1)/2 & n \text{ odd}, \end{cases} \qquad f^{-1}(z) = \begin{cases} 2z & z \ge 0, \\ -2z - 1 & z < 0. \end{cases}$$
So $|\mathbb{Z}| = |\mathbb{N}|$, even though ℤ is "infinite in both directions." Listing is about order of *visiting*, not order of *size*.

**Pairs.** Now the buses. Label passengers by pairs $(b, s)$: bus $b$, seat $s$, with $b, s \in \mathbb{N}$. Is $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ countable? It looks two-dimensionally infinite. Listing row by row fails: you'd never finish row 0. The fix is to walk the grid in **diagonals**: first all pairs with $x + y = 0$, then $x + y = 1$, then $x + y = 2$, and so on. Each diagonal is finite, so every pair is reached after finitely many steps.

> **Definition (Cantor pairing function).**
> $$\pi(x, y) = \frac{(x + y)(x + y + 1)}{2} + y.$$

The first term counts how many pairs lie on earlier diagonals ($1 + 2 + \cdots + (x + y) = T_{x+y}$, a triangular number), and $+\,y$ says how far along its own diagonal $(x, y)$ sits.

> **Theorem.** $\pi : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ is a bijection. Hence $|\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{N}|$.

**Proof.** Write $T_w = w(w+1)/2$ for the $w$-th triangular number, so $\pi(x, y) = T_{x+y} + y$, and note $T_{w+1} - T_w = w + 1$.

*Every number is hit exactly once.* Fix $z \in \mathbb{N}$. Since $T_0 = 0$ and the $T_w$ increase without bound, there is exactly one $w \ge 0$ with
$$T_w \le z < T_{w+1} = T_w + w + 1.$$
Any pair with $\pi(x, y) = z$ must have $x + y = w$: if $x + y = w'$, then $T_{w'} \le \pi(x, y) = T_{w'} + y \le T_{w'} + w' < T_{w'+1}$, and the uniqueness of $w$ forces $w' = w$. Then $y = z - T_w$ is determined, and $x = w - y$. Conversely, $y = z - T_w$ satisfies $0 \le y \le w$, so $x = w - y \ge 0$ and $(x, y)$ is a genuine pair with $\pi(x, y) = T_w + y = z$. So each $z$ has exactly one preimage. ∎

The proof hands us an inverse: given $z$, find the largest $w$ with $T_w \le z$ (explicitly, $w = \left\lfloor \frac{\sqrt{8z + 1} - 1}{2} \right\rfloor$), then $y = z - T_w$ and $x = w - y$.

**Hotel solution:** treat the current guests as "bus 0" (the guest in room $s$ is passenger $s$ of bus 0), and number the arriving buses $1, 2, 3, \ldots$. Then passenger $s$ of bus $b$ goes to room $\pi(b, s)$. Every person gets exactly one room and every room gets exactly one person. Countably many countable crowds fit in one countable hotel.

### 4. The rationals: the Calkin–Wilf tree

The rationals feel even bigger. They are *dense*: between any two there are infinitely many more (↩ S1E07). Yet:

> **Theorem (Cantor, 1874 and later).** ℚ is countable.

The quickest proof: each positive rational is $p/q$ in lowest terms, and $p/q \mapsto \pi(p, q)$ is an injection into ℕ, so $|\mathbb{Q}_{>0}| \le |\mathbb{N}|$. To upgrade "injection both ways" to a bijection one uses the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem (if $|A| \le |B|$ and $|B| \le |A|$ then $|A| = |B|$). But there is a far prettier construction that lists every positive rational **exactly once**, with no skipping and no theorem needed.

> **Definition (Calkin–Wilf tree).** Start with $\frac11$ at the root. Every node $\frac ab$ has two children:
> $$\text{left child } \frac{a}{a + b}, \qquad \text{right child } \frac{a + b}{b}.$$
> The **Calkin–Wilf sequence** reads the tree row by row, left to right.

The first rows:
```
                      1/1
            1/2                  2/1
       1/3       3/2        2/3       3/1
     1/4 4/3   3/5 5/2    2/5 5/3   3/4 4/1
```
So the sequence begins
$$\tfrac11, \tfrac12, \tfrac21, \tfrac13, \tfrac32, \tfrac23, \tfrac31, \tfrac14, \tfrac43, \tfrac35, \tfrac52, \tfrac25, \tfrac53, \tfrac34, \tfrac41, \ldots$$

> **Theorem (Calkin–Wilf, 2000).** Every positive rational number appears in the Calkin–Wilf tree exactly once, and in lowest terms.

**Proof.**
*Lowest terms.* $\gcd(a, a+b) = \gcd(a, b)$ and $\gcd(a+b, b) = \gcd(a, b)$, since any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $a+b$, and conversely. The root has $\gcd(1, 1) = 1$, so by induction down the tree every entry is in lowest terms.

*Every fraction appears.* Note that a left child $\frac{a}{a+b}$ is less than 1 and a right child $\frac{a+b}{b}$ is greater than 1. So a fraction $\frac pq \ne \frac11$ in lowest terms can only be a child of one possible parent:
$$\text{if } p < q:\ \text{parent } \frac{p}{q - p}; \qquad \text{if } p > q:\ \text{parent } \frac{p - q}{q}.$$
(It can't be that $p = q$ unless $\frac pq = \frac11$, by lowest terms.) Each step reduces $p + q$, so repeatedly taking parents reaches a fraction with $p = q$, i.e. $\frac11$, after finitely many steps. Reversing the chain shows $\frac pq$ is in the tree. Formally, this is strong induction on $p + q$.

*Only once.* First, $\frac11$ occurs only at the root, because every child is either less than 1 (left) or greater than 1 (right). Now suppose some fraction occurs at two different nodes, and choose such a fraction $\frac pq$ with $p + q$ as small as possible. Neither occurrence is the root, so both are children, and both are left children (if $p < q$) or both right children (if $p > q$). Their parent nodes carry the same value, given by the parent rule. If the two parent nodes were the same node, our two occurrences would be the same child of it, which they are not. So the parent value occurs at two different nodes, and its numerator plus denominator is smaller than $p + q$. That contradicts minimality. ∎

So the Calkin–Wilf sequence is a bijection between ℕ (positions) and ℚ$_{>0}$. Interleave it with negatives and 0, exactly as we did for ℤ, and ℚ is countable.

A delightful bonus (due to Moshe Newman): each term of the sequence can be computed from the previous one alone,
$$x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2\lfloor x_n \rfloor - x_n + 1}.$$

### 5. Payoff, and a sharper question

Answer to the cold-open question: there are *exactly as many* even numbers as natural numbers, as many integers, as many pairs, and as many fractions. The density of ℚ, which made it feel "bigger," is about how the rationals are *arranged* on the line, not about how many there are. Size, defined by matching, ignores arrangement.

The hotel can absorb one guest, infinitely many guests, and infinitely many buses of infinitely many guests. Is there *any* crowd it can't absorb? Is every infinite set countable?

That's the question for ↪ S1E12, and the answer will be one of the most surprising theorems in mathematics.

**Zoom out (thread: bijections are the real way to count).** The move of this episode, "two things are the same if you can match them perfectly," will come back with more structure attached. A group isomorphism (↪ S1E17) is a bijection that respects the operation. A change of basis in Season 2 is a bijection that respects addition and scaling. Each time, "same" means "matchable, keeping the structure."

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Pairing and unpairing

**(a) Compute $\pi(3, 4)$.** Here $x + y = 7$, so
$$\pi(3, 4) = \frac{7 \cdot 8}{2} + 4 = 28 + 4 = 32.$$

**(b) Which pair has code 100?** Find the largest $w$ with $T_w = w(w+1)/2 \le 100$: $T_{12} = 78$, $T_{13} = 91$, $T_{14} = 105 > 100$. So $w = 13$ (formula check: $\sqrt{801} \approx 28.30$, $(28.30 - 1)/2 \approx 13.65$, floor 13). Then
$$y = 100 - 91 = 9, \qquad x = 13 - 9 = 4.$$
Check: $\pi(4, 9) = \frac{13 \cdot 14}{2} + 9 = 91 + 9 = 100$. ✓

**(c) The grid.** The first values, rows $x = 0..4$, columns $y = 0..4$:

| $x \backslash y$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 2 | 5 | 9 | 14 |
| 1 | 1 | 4 | 8 | 13 | 19 |
| 2 | 3 | 7 | 12 | 18 | 25 |
| 3 | 6 | 11 | 17 | 24 | 32 |
| 4 | 10 | 16 | 23 | 31 | 40 |

Each anti-diagonal holds consecutive numbers ($3, 4, 5$ on the diagonal $x + y = 2$, then $6, 7, 8, 9$), confirming the zig-zag reading order.

**(d) The buses.** If the current guests are "bus 0" and arriving bus $k$ is relabeled bus $k$ (for $k \ge 1$), then passenger in seat 3 of bus 2 goes to room $\pi(2, 3) = \frac{5 \cdot 6}{2} + 3 = 18$, and the guest who was in room 5 (bus 0, seat 5) goes to room $\pi(0, 5) = 15 + 5 = 20$.

### Example 2: Finding 3/5 in the Calkin–Wilf list

**Climb to the root** using the parent rule:
- $\frac35$: $3 < 5$, so it is a **left** child of $\frac{3}{5 - 3} = \frac32$.
- $\frac32$: $3 > 2$, so it is a **right** child of $\frac{3 - 2}{2} = \frac12$.
- $\frac12$: $1 < 2$, so it is a **left** child of $\frac{1}{2 - 1} = \frac11$.

Path from the root: Left, Right, Left.

**Convert to a position.** Number the tree positions 1, 2, 3, … row by row (root = 1). Write a 1 for the root, then append 0 for each Left and 1 for each Right: $1\,0\,1\,0$ in binary, which is $10$. So $\frac35$ is the **10th** term (counting from 1). Check against the list above: $\frac11, \frac12, \frac21, \frac13, \frac32, \frac23, \frac31, \frac14, \frac43, \frac35$. ✓ (This works because the children of position $k$ sit at positions $2k$ and $2k + 1$, as in a binary heap.)

**Next term by Newman's formula.** With $x = \frac35$, $\lfloor x \rfloor = 0$, so
$$x_{\text{next}} = \frac{1}{0 - \frac35 + 1} = \frac{1}{\frac25} = \frac52,$$
which is the 11th term in the list. ✓ One more: $x = \frac52$, $\lfloor x \rfloor = 2$, so $\frac{1}{4 - \frac52 + 1} = \frac{1}{\frac52} = \frac25$. ✓

### Example 3: The integers, with inverse checked

Using $f(n) = n/2$ (even $n$), $-(n+1)/2$ (odd $n$):

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $f(n)$ | 0 | −1 | 1 | −2 | 2 | −3 | 3 |

Inverse check for $z = -3$: $z < 0$, so $f^{-1}(-3) = -2(-3) - 1 = 5$, and $f(5) = -(5 + 1)/2 = -3$. ✓ For $z = 3$: $f^{-1}(3) = 6$ and $f(6) = 3$. ✓

*Why it's a bijection:* even $n$ go to $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ bijectively ($n \mapsto n/2$), odd $n$ go to $\{-1, -2, \ldots\}$ bijectively ($n \mapsto -(n+1)/2$), and these two target sets partition ℤ.

### Example 4 (conceptual): the hotel can't always do it by shifting

Suppose infinitely many buses arrive, and the manager first moves the current guests to the even rooms, then tries to seat the buses "one bus at a time": bus 1 takes rooms $1, 3, 5, 7, \ldots$ in order, and bus 2 is to start once bus 1 is seated. Bus 1 alone uses up *every* odd room, so bus 2 never gets a room. Any plan that seats an entire infinite bus before starting the next one fails in the same way. The pairing function succeeds exactly because it interleaves: each diagonal takes finitely many people from finitely many buses. This is the same lesson as "list ℕ × ℕ by diagonals, not by rows," and it is the key design principle for every countability proof.

## Common misconceptions
- **Misconception:** "There are half as many even numbers as natural numbers." **Reality:** *density* (the proportion among $0, \ldots, N$) is $\frac12$, but cardinality is the same. These are different measurements of "size."
- **Misconception:** "Since ℚ is dense, it must be uncountable." **Reality:** density is about order and arrangement; countability is about matching. ℚ is both dense and countable.
- **Misconception:** "A bijection must preserve order." **Reality:** the listing $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, \ldots$ scrambles the order of ℤ completely. Cardinality forgets all structure except "which element is which." (Ordinals, in ↪ S1E13, remember the order, and there the answers change.)
- **Misconception:** "∞ + 1 > ∞, so the hotel can't fit one more guest." **Reality:** "∞" isn't a single number here; the statement $|\mathbb{N}| + 1 = |\mathbb{N}|$ for cardinals is exactly what the room shift proves.
- **Misconception:** "If I can't find a bijection, the sets have different sizes." **Reality:** failing to find one proves nothing. Showing *no* bijection exists takes a real argument; that is ↪ S1E12.

- **Core (conceptual):** Show that the union of two countably infinite sets is countable, and that $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ is countable. (Hint: apply $\pi$ twice.)
- **Stretch (proof):** Prove that the set of all *finite* sequences of natural numbers is countable. Deduce that the set of polynomials with integer coefficients is countable, and hence (Cantor, 1874) that the set of real algebraic numbers is countable.

## Historical note
Galileo Galilei raised the squares paradox in his *Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences* (1638), through his character Salviati, and concluded that infinite quantities cannot be compared. Bernard Bolzano's *Paradoxes of the Infinite* (published posthumously in 1851) took the matching idea further but still found it paradoxical.

Georg Cantor turned the paradox into a theory. In his 1874 paper "On a Property of the Collection of All Real Algebraic Numbers," he proved that the algebraic numbers can be listed (so they form a countable set) while the real numbers cannot, the first proof that infinities come in different sizes. Dedekind, in correspondence with Cantor in 1873, helped with the argument for the algebraic numbers. The diagonal-walk enumeration of pairs and of rationals appears in Cantor's later work; the polynomial formula for the pairing is now named after him.

The hotel is due to David Hilbert, who used the story in lectures on the infinite in the mid-1920s; it reached a wide audience through George Gamow's popular book *One Two Three… Infinity* (1947). The Calkin–Wilf tree is surprisingly recent: Neil Calkin and Herbert Wilf published "Recounting the Rationals" in 2000, though the underlying sequence (Stern's diatomic sequence, whose consecutive terms give the numerators and denominators) goes back to Moritz Stern in 1858.
